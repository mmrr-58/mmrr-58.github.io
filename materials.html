There's this type of table that I've been seeing everywhere: in cafés, on the uni campus, in the corner of every interior design post I've ever scrolled past. A thin sheet of metal, usually painted matte black. In a photo, it looks perfect. Clean lines, no visual noise, the kind of object that makes a room look tasteful.

Then you sit at one.

The edge meets your forearms in exactly the wrong place. In winter, the surface is cold enough that you pull your sleeves over your wrists before resting them on it. Put a mug down and it screeches. Lean on a corner and you find out how sharp a corner can be. None of this is dramatic. Nobody gets hurt. But every interaction with the table is a little worse than it needed to be.

Interactions in the physical world

I think this happens because materials are often chosen at the end of the process, and chosen by eye. We pick metal because it looks precise, glass because it looks elegant, concrete because it looks robust. Those are real qualities, but they're qualities of an image. A table isn't supposed to be an image. It's supposed to be a table.

The material is the first thing you interact with. Before you understand what an object does, you already know how it feels: whether it's warm or cold, rough or smooth, whether it gives a little under pressure or pushes back. Wood warms up under your hands. Fabric absorbs sound. Rounded edges let your body rest. They're an interface of sorts for the real world.

In UX we talk a lot about affordances, the cues that tell you how something can be used. Materials are full of them. A soft surface invites you to lean. A cold, hard one tells you to keep your distance. When the material says "keep your distance" on an object whose whole purpose is to be used up close, the design is contradicting itself.

Something that feels good

I'm not against beautiful things. Aesthetics matter, and they're part of why people choose to live with an object in the first place. My problem is sacrificing everything else for the sake of aesthetics. A very deep regret of mine is getting something that looks good, but I don't use because it doesn't feel good.

Thinking about materials

What I'd like to see, and what I try to do in my own work, is materials being part of the conversation from the beginning. That means asking a few questions alongside the usual ones about form and function:

  • What temperature will this be when someone touches it?
  • What does it sound like when it's used?
  • Where will hands, arms and bodies rest on it, and what will they feel there?
  • How will it age, and will it still be pleasant to use in five years?

Answering these doesn't mean giving up on aesthetics. Often the answer is a small change: a rounded edge instead of a sharp fold, a wooden top on a metal frame, a coating that takes the chill off. The table can still look clean. It just also has to feel like it wants to be used.

This goes beyond furniture, too. Working across physical computing and interface design, I see the same mistake on screens: interfaces chosen for how they look in a portfolio shot rather than how they feel under a thumb. Digital materials like transparency, depth and motion have textures of their own, and they deserve the same care.

The test

The easiest test I know is to stop looking and start touching. Close your eyes, put your hands on the thing you've designed, and use it the way someone would on an ordinary Tuesday. If it feels good, the material is doing its job. If you find yourself pulling your sleeves over your wrists, it isn't.

We design things to be used. The material is the part people actually touch.
